It Is Vital That He Be · The Minutes

PREPARE IN 1 MINUTE

The story: A tiny lighthouse museum on the coast of Maine.

The grammar: A demand in formal English: THAT + the bare verb, for everyone (It is vital that he be present).

Short class (about 52 min): I You and Meetings · II The Treasurer’s Notes · III The Charter, 1974 · IV Around the Table · V Demand or Fact? · VI Unpopular Opinions · VII Your Formal Voice · IX Your Motion. Skip if you’re short on time: VIII The Newspaper Calls (marked EXTRA on your screen). On the practice pages the student speaks first and taps on their own screen. Full lesson: about 59 min.

Open with: “What meetings do you go to? Which one is the most formal?”

A tiny lighthouse museum on the coast of Maine. Thursday, 7 PM: its eight trustees meet, because a company wants to buy the lighthouse and turn it into a hotel. The student is the new board secretary. The treasurer sends his demands like text messages; the minutes need them in formal English. After words of demand, advice and importance (vital, essential, insist, recommend, suggest, request, propose) + that, formal English uses the bare verb for every subject: It is vital that he be present. We recommend that she take the chair. The negative is not + bare verb; the passive is be + participle. The turn: the 1974 charter says the founders insisted that the light be kept burning. The company’s lawyer reads it as a fact; the verb says it was a demand, and it still stands.

THE COMMON ERRORS

Many languages have full subjunctive endings, so students look for one and fall back on that she takes, will / would, suggest him to wait or that he doesn’t sign. English has no endings to learn: only the bare verb, the same for everyone. The hard part is noticing when to use it.

ON SCREEN

The minutes book: the treasurer’s handwritten note on the left, the formal line in ink on the right. The frozen verb (scene III): tap a subject and watch the verb stay the same. The lighthouse lamp (scene IV): every motion lights a panel. Scene V stamps DEMAND or FACT.

The Treasurer’s Notes (II): six casual notes on the left and six formal lines on the right. The student taps a note, then the line that says the same thing (a choice of meaning: the formal lines show the rule before it is explained). Every right pair goes into the minutes book in ink; the student reads the line out loud, slowly, like a secretary. At the end, ask the question on the screen: why BE and not IS? Let them guess.

The Charter, 1974 (III): the lighthouse charter, on dark paper. The student reads it out loud like a lawyer and taps every verb after THAT (be kept, live, be sold, read, not change). Every word can be tapped; was built is a trap: a fact, not a demand. Then they finish the rule on the right by choosing inside each line (live, be, not change, be kept, that she take, a fact). Under it, the frozen verb. The rule then stays in the corner of IV.

Around the Table (IV): six voices. Read each one in character (the clue shows it and the first words). The student says the formal line, then taps Check: the model appears and a panel of the lamp lights up. The last voice is the company’s lawyer reading the charter as a story; the student answers with the demand: the light be kept burning.

A NOTE ON REGISTER

British English often uses should (that he should be present) or even the indicative here. That is not wrong, and you can say so. The lesson trains the formal American form, which exams, contracts and minutes expect.

UNPOPULAR OPINIONS (VI) AND THE SURPRISE QUESTION

After Demand or Fact, four opinions the board doesn’t want to hear (a hotel instead of the museum, meetings without an agenda, messages after 7 PM, the oldest person’s final word). Your student taps a number from 1 to 5 and defends it in the formal voice (I’d insist that it be…); you take the opposite number. There is no right answer. Wait five seconds in silence, then use the PUSH line (only you see it) to make it harder. Recast quietly while they talk (that he be, not that he is); at the end ask them to fix one or two sentences themselves.

The Surprise question button (bottom right, only you see it) opens one personal question at any moment. Use it when the lesson has been only exercises for a while.

WOLF IN THIS LESSON

  • Out loud: I You and Meetings, IV Around the Table (you read every voice), VI Unpopular Opinions, VII Your Formal Voice, VIII The Newspaper Calls (you are the reporter; no longer extra).
  • Focus: II The Treasurer’s Notes, III The Charter, 1974, V Demand or Fact?
  • Write: IX, three formal demands for the student’s building, street or city. They go to their My notes.
