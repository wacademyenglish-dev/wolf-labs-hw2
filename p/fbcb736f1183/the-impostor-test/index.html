The Impostor Test · The Voice Line

A question tag copies the auxiliary and the person from the sentence and flips the sign: You did the work, didn’t you? · It wasn’t luck, was it? Then the voice decides: a falling tag tells and asks for agreement; a rising tag really asks. Students put isn’t it? on everything, or don’t you? after BE. The theme is the feeling of being an impostor at work: the tags we hang on our own achievements. On screen, every tag sentence gets a neon voice line that falls or rises at the end; with the tag off, the line goes flat.

SAY IT TWICE

Every tag sentence gets said twice: once falling, once rising. Exaggerate the melody. The corrector can check the tag; only your ear can check the direction.

NOT A DIAGNOSIS

The situations in scene V and the compliments in scene VI are conversation, not a test of personality. In scene VI you pay the compliment; your student answers without thinking; you tap the shape.

THE RECORDINGS

Three short parts about a 2019 review (62 studies, 14,000 people): the number of people who feel like impostors went from 9 to 82 in 100, because every study used a different test. Each plays with pause and 0.75×; the transcript opens after listening.

YOU ARE THREE PEOPLE

Scene VIII: a falling tag gets “Yes, that’s right.”; a rising one gets real, new information.

THE ARENA (IX) AND THE SURPRISE QUESTION

Scene IX is now an arena. The biggest part of a project was your student’s: do they say it flat, or keep a tag at the end? They pick a side and defend it for one minute with tags (“Nobody will say it for you, will they?”); then they tap Swap sides and defend the other one. There is no right answer. Wait five seconds in silence before they start, and use the PUSH line (only you see it) to make it harder. Recast mistakes quietly while they talk; at the end, ask them to fix one or two sentences themselves.

The Surprise question button (bottom right, only you see it) opens one personal question at any moment. Use it when the lesson has been only exercises for a while.
