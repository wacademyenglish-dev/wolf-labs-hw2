It Wasn’t the Time · Rack Focus

PREPARE IN 1 MINUTE

The story: A fact-checking hour about focus and multitasking.

The grammar: Two Shapes: English has two ways to put the focus on one part of a sentence.

Short class (about 46 min): I Interrupted · II Two Shapes · III Rack Focus · IV Sixty-Three · V Five out of Two Hundred · VI The Missing It · X What You Lost. Skip if you’re short on time: VII, VIII, IX (marked EXTRA on your screen). Full lesson: about 67 min.

Open with: “The average self-rating, on a scale where fifty is average, was …”

A fact-checking hour about focus and multitasking. The student tests famous sentences (every interruption costs you 23 minutes, our attention span is shorter than a goldfish’s) against what the studies really show. The language is the language of correction and emphasis: cleft sentences. It was the conversation that slowed the drivers. What they got wrong was themselves. On screen, a camera viewfinder: the student pulls the focus to one part of a sentence, that part goes sharp, the rest goes soft, and the sentence rebuilds itself as a cleft. The hour doesn’t teach that multitasking is bad; it teaches the difference between backed, not shown and misquoted.

THE EMPTY IT

In It was X that …, IT means nothing; it only holds the place of the subject. Many students drop it: Was the conversation that … Ask: what is the subject of this sentence? Scene II: the student reads a fact-checker’s note, taps the words that move the focus and finishes the rule (the rule stays in view). IV: sort six lines into SHE SAID IT / SHE DID NOT. V: match each question to its number. VI: find the broken part of six headlines and say the fix. VII: tap the stamp, then say the correction.

RACK FOCUS

Scene III is the heart of the grammar. Each part the student focuses makes a different cleft and a different correction. Ask every time: so what was it NOT?

THE FACTS

23 minutes 15 seconds: said in interviews, not in the paper it is credited to. Supertaskers: 5 of 200 drivers lost nothing while talking on a phone (2.5%). Self-rating: 277 people, average 63 where 50 is average. 2009 media-multitasking study: later teams could not find the effect again. Goldfish: the 8-second number came from a statistics website quoted in a marketing report, not from brain research.

THE RECORDINGS

Two recordings, each with pause and 0.75×; the transcript opens after listening. Scene VIII: the student taps KEEP or STRIKE on each line; don’t play the speaker as a joke; the scene only works if the voice sounds convincing.

THE DEBATE

Scene IX: you and the student take opposite sides (a 45-second clock for each round), and after round two you swap. Tap each shape you hear; the dashed buttons are the classic mistakes, and the student says it again.

UNPOPULAR OPINIONS (VI) AND THE SURPRISE QUESTION

After the simulator numbers, your student rates four unpopular opinions about focus from 1 to 5 and defends the number with a cleft. There is no right answer: wait five seconds in silence, then take the opposite number and use the PUSH line (only you see it) to make it harder. Recast mistakes quietly while they talk; at the end, ask them to fix one or two sentences themselves.

The Surprise question button (bottom right, only you see it) opens one personal question at any moment. Use it when the lesson has been only exercises for a while.

WOLF IN THIS LESSON

  • Out loud: I Interrupted, III Rack Focus, VI Unpopular Opinions, VII The Claims Court, IX The Debate.
  • Listen: IV Sixty-Three, VIII The Confident Voice.
  • Focus: II Two Shapes, V Five out of Two Hundred.
  • Write: X, what they lost in scene I, as a cleft. It goes to their My notes.
