You Needn’t Have Bothered · Did You Do It?

PREPARE IN 1 MINUTE

The story: December 28th.

The grammar: Priya’s message first (II): tap the six verb phrases, finish the rule. Then ask “Did you do it?” after every line.

Short class (about 46 min): I You and Your Bags · II The Unpacking · III Six Traps · IV Snowed In · V The Hot Seat · VI The Owner Calls · VII Traveling Light. Full lesson: about 46 min.

Open with: “Are you a heavy packer or a light packer? What always goes in your bag?”

December 28th. Four friends drive up to a cabin in the mountains for New Year’s. The car is packed to the roof: three camping stoves, a generator, forty cans of beans, a karaoke machine. The cabin already has all of it. And the two things that mattered, the snow chains and the matches, are still in a garage two hundred miles away. The grammar: needn’t have + participle (we DID it, and it was not necessary) against didn’t need to + verb (it was not necessary, usually so we didn’t), and the chance never taken: could have + participle / would have been able to.

ON SCREEN

One question becomes the board: “Did you do it?” Every right sentence lands in a lane: amber, like the cabin window, for what they did for nothing; snow white for what they didn’t need to do; a dashed lane for the missed chance. Ask the question out loud after every line.

THE NEW PAGES

  • II The Unpacking: Priya’s message to her sister. The student taps the six verb phrases (each one lands in its lane) and finishes the rule. The rule then stays in the corner.
  • III Six Traps: one part of each line is wrong; the student says the right words, then Check.
  • IV Snowed In: you read the moment; the student finishes it out loud first, then Check. Ask the question under the answer.

THE COMMON ERRORS

Students translate word by word and produce didn’t need to have bought. In English, have + participle belongs to needn’t; didn’t need to takes the base verb. The second trap is meaning: needn’t have always says you DID it. Also needn’t to, needn’t have bring, mustn’t have (a guess, not wasted effort), could buy for a past chance.

A NOTE

In the old version, “didn’t need to pack twelve blankets” described something they did. This version only uses didn’t need to for things nobody did (the snacks, the roof box, the shovel, the sleigh).

THE HOT SEAT (V) AND THE SURPRISE QUESTION

Scene V is now a hot seat: the student is Leo, who packed the car, and you are the group at breakfast. Ask the four questions in character; your secret (you forgot the matches) is on your screen only, so let Leo find it. There is no right answer: wait five seconds in silence after each question, and use the PUSH line (only you see it) to make Leo turn the questions back on you. At the end the student chooses how Leo makes up for it, on the screen. Ask “Did you do it?” after their lines, recast quietly while they talk, and at the end ask them to fix one or two sentences themselves.

The Surprise question button (only you see it) opens one personal question at any moment. Use it when the lesson has been only exercises for a while.

WOLF IN THIS LESSON

  • Out loud: I You and Your Bags, IV Snowed In, V The Hot Seat (you are the group), VI The Owner Calls (you are the owner).
  • Focus: II The Unpacking, III Six Traps.
  • Write: VII, the student’s last trip in three lines. They go to their My notes.
