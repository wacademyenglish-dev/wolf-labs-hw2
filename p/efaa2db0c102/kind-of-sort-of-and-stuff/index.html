Kind Of, Sort Of, and Stuff · Soft Focus

PREPARE IN 1 MINUTE

The story: Friday, 9:12 PM, Denver.

The grammar: The Rule: Vague is not wrong. It depends on who is listening.

Short class (about 51 min): I You and Talking · III Trick Questions · V Voice Memos to Her Sister · VI The Rule · VII Carousel 5 · VIII The Passenger Council · X Two Versions. Skip if you’re short on time: IX (marked EXTRA on your screen). Full lesson: about 58 min.

Open with: “Voice memos or text messages? Who do you send them to?”

Friday, 9:12 PM, Denver. A traveler lands, waits at carousel 5 for forty-seven minutes, and her suitcase never comes out. She tells the same night twice: to her sister in voice memos (I landed at nine-ish, it’s kind of big and bluish, my coat and stuff are in there) and to the lost baggage desk on a claim form (9:12 PM, a navy blue hard-shell suitcase, 28 inches). The grammar: kind of / sort of + adjective or verb, kind of a + noun, and stuff / and things like that / or something (like that), -ish, a bit + adjective and a bit of a + noun. The point: vague language is not lazy English. It is how people talk to people close to them. What matters is who is listening.

THE COMMON ERRORS

Many C1 students avoid vague language and sound stiff, or translate it: a kind of weird, and stuffs, and thing, or something like, nine-ish o’clock, a bit of tired, kind of very. Some give the exact version to the sister too, and sound like a police report: accept it, then ask for a more relaxed memo.

ON SCREEN

Two screens: the voice memo (a wave, and the vague words glowing soft and out of focus) and the claim form (sharp, monospaced fields). Scene V: the student reads the exact fact on the claim form, says the memo out loud and taps Check; the vague words glow on the voice memo. Scene VI: four clues, the student guesses what the lines share, then finds the broken part of five lines. Scene VII: the student taps each answer and then who is listening, the desk or her sister; afterwards say each answer again in the other voice.

THE VIDEO

Scene III: a street interview in Washington, D.C. (about six minutes), sealed until the first play, in four chapters. Real people, trick questions, no script. Before the video the student chooses how to answer a trick question and slides a ruler. In each chapter the student taps True or Lie (▶ plays the line again); in chapter three the student says how sure each person is. After the video: one more ruler.

The words (II): questions, highest, guess, discovered and direction, before the video, in hear it in the video and hot seat. Five minutes: let the student guess first. In the video (with CC on), these words turn yellow; the explanation comes after the video, in Line by Line. Line by Line (IV, extra): every line of the video; tap one to hear it again, and the lines with yellow words open the explanation.

THE PASSENGER COUNCIL (VIII) AND THE SURPRISE QUESTION

After Carousel 5, your student sits on the passenger council and votes three proposals about lost bags: yes, propose a change, or no. There is no right answer. Let them say why the casual way (kind of, a bit of a, -ish), wait five seconds in silence, then use the PUSH line (only you see it): it often asks for the exact version, for the minutes. The last word is their own proposal, one minute, casual then exact; you vote against it. Recast quietly while they talk; at the end ask them to fix one or two sentences themselves. This page replaces Your Two Versions.

The Surprise question button (bottom right, only you see it) opens one personal question at any moment. Use it when the lesson has been only exercises for a while.
