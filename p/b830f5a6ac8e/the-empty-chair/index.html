The Empty Chair · The Calendar

In April, Maya asked her manager, Daniel, for a conversation. He moved it three times. In July she gave her notice, and the conversation never happened. Her chair is still in the meeting room. The hour uses the empty chair technique: the student talks to someone who is not there, then changes chairs and answers as that person. The language is the past of advice and possibility: should have (it was your job), could have (it was a chance), shouldn’t have (you did it, and it was wrong) and couldn’t have (it was impossible). On screen, a desk calendar from April to July with the meeting stamped MOVED three times, a door that closes to move advice into the past, and a filing cabinet with three drawers: DUTY, CHANCE, IMPOSSIBLE.

THE QUESTION THAT DECIDES

Should or could? Ask one thing: was anybody counting on you? Yes: should have. No, it was only open: could have. Never possible: couldn’t have.

THE WARM-UP COMES BACK

Scene I: tap the form you hear for the first half of each answer (only you see the buttons). Most students say she should do about last week. Don’t correct; the count comes back in scene X.

THE RECORDINGS

Five recordings, each with pause and 0.75×; the transcript opens after listening. Voice 3 can go in DUTY or CHANCE: ask which one they would say. Voice 4 is the other direction: the founder did it (shouldn’t have).

THE PHYSICAL SCENE

Scene VII asks the student to stand up and change chairs, really. Online, they move to another chair in the room. Don’t skip it: the change of seat is what makes the second voice come out. Let them speak to the chair: “you”, not “she”.

KEEP IT SAFE

The regrets in this hour are about work, study and small decisions. If the student brings something heavy, listen, then go back to Daniel and Maya.

THE LATE PROJECT (VIII) AND THE SURPRISE QUESTION

A work negotiation after the empty chair: the student runs a project that was ten days late, and you are Ms. Lane, the client. Ask the four questions, wait five seconds in silence, and only accept answers that own the mistake (we should have, I couldn’t have …, but I could have). Your secret limit is on your screen only: start at 30% off and give in only for a clear “we should have”. Use the PUSH line to test their limit, then close the deal on screen. Recast mistakes quietly; at the end, ask them to fix one or two sentences themselves.

The Surprise question button (bottom right, only you see it) opens one personal question at any moment. Use it when the lesson has been only exercises for a while.
