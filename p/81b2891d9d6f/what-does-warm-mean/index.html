What Does Warm Mean? · The Office · The Dundies

PREPARE IN 1 MINUTE

The story: A boss who means well, a fire drill, and a lot of tiny trophies.

The grammar: Absolutely, Not Very: I wasn’t very scared. I was absolutely terrified.

Short class (about 51 min): I Warm-up and The Interview · III The Clip · IV Scene by Scene · V The Deal · VI Absolutely, Not Very · VII The Dundies · IX One Minute. Skip if you’re short on time: VIII (marked EXTRA on your screen). Full lesson: about 58 min.

Open with: “You know Dwight now. In the clip, how do people feel when the smoke comes? Guess.”

The Office (US, 2005–2013), a comedy about a paper company, filmed like a documentary. Dwight gave a fire safety talk and nobody listened, so he starts a fire in the office himself and locks the doors: smoke, panic, people climbing out of windows. Then he says there is no fire, it was only a simulation. The grammar: extreme adjectives: absolutely / really / totally + extreme (absolutely terrified, exhausted, starving, boiling, freezing, furious, hilarious, awful) and very / a bit / extremely / too + normal (very scared, tired, hungry, hot). An extreme word never takes very, and a normal word never takes absolutely.

A note for the teacher: the video is the official Peacock compilation “Most Watched Cold Opens”, and the lesson plays only 35:12 to 39:02 (the fire drill). Right after 39:02 comes a CPR joke about a character’s skin color, which is not part of this lesson. Two words are bleeped. Lines checked against the subtitles (24/09).

Warm-up and The Interview (I): two parts. 1 · Warm-up: three questions about the student’s life, one at a time (big on the screen, with “Ask more” under each and one short line about the show under the photo). Let them talk; don’t correct the grammar yet. 2 · The Interview: you are Dwight Schrute: the card with his facts shows only on your screen (dashed border). The student asks questions and taps each one they ask; answer in character, extremely seriously, with extreme adjectives (absolutely essential, totally exhausted). Then the student guesses who you are, and the photo shows the answer.

The words (II): trick, serious, crazy, notice and sales, before the video, in turn the cards and hot seat. Five minutes: let the student guess first. In the video (with CC on), these words turn yellow; the explanation comes after the video, in Line by Line. Line by Line (IV) lists the lines with the yellow words: tap a line, hear it again, and the student says it like the character.

The ending (IX, One Minute): the student picks a topic (the worst day of the year, the best holiday, Dwight’s fire drill) and talks for one minute with extreme adjectives, no very with them. Then the teacher. Speaking only.

ON SCREEN

The cover is the office at night. I: Warm-up and The Interview. III: the clip in three steps. Before: two questions to tap, then say why. Watch: One word is wrong (say the right one) and What happens? (tap the answer), each line with a ▶ time: tap it and the video jumps to that moment. After: put the five scenes of the drill in order, then retell it with extreme words. IV: five frames on the film strip. V, The Deal: the student speaks for the office, the teacher is Dwight; three questions in role, then a deal about the next drill. VI: sort six words into two boxes, very + … and absolutely + …; when all six are right, the rule appears. VII, The Dundies: a shelf with six gold plastic trophies, an empty plaque on each. The student reads the category and the clue, says the sentence out loud, then taps Check: the right sentence is written and the trophy lights up with its plaque. At six, the fake audience goes wild. VIII, Turn It Up: match six normal words to their extreme words, then say both. IX: one minute of talking.

THE COMMON ERRORS

very terrified / exhausted / starving / hilarious, absolutely scared / angry / cold / funny, a bit exhausted, extremely boiling, too boiling, more starving, a very awful idea.

THE DEAL (V) AND THE SURPRISE QUESTION

Right after Line by Line, a negotiation. The student speaks for the office after the fire drill; you are Dwight. The student wants no more surprise drills; you want one every month. Your secret limit is on your screen only: you can accept announced drills, but you want one surprise a year. First ask Dwight’s three questions (how did you feel? was my talk good? why should I tell you the date?) and let the student answer in role with extreme adjectives (absolutely terrified, very angry). Then negotiate and close the deal on the screen. Wait five seconds in silence before they start, and use the PUSH line to make the deal harder. Recast quietly while they talk (very terrified, absolutely tired, more freezing); at the end, ask them to fix one or two sentences themselves. About seven minutes.

The Surprise question button (bottom right, only you see it) opens one personal question at any moment. Use it when the lesson has been only exercises for a while.
