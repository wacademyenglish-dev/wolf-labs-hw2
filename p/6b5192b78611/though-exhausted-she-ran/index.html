Though Exhausted, She Ran · The Elevation Profile

PREPARE IN 1 MINUTE

The story: Dee Carver, 44, drives a school bus.

The grammar: The Rule: Cut the subject and the verb. Keep the rest.

Short class (about 54 min): I You and the Long Run · II The Race Notes · III The Rule · IV The Cut · V The Editor Calls · VI The Contract · VII Your Hard Miles · VIII The Finish Line. Full lesson: about 54 min.

Open with: “Do you run, walk or play any sport? How far is “far” for you?”

Dee Carver, 44, drives a school bus. At 2 AM on a cold Sunday she stands at the start line of her first marathon, one headlamp among nine thousand, and at 6:28 AM she crosses the finish line under the lighthouse as the sun comes up. Her race notes are plain: I was exhausted. I kept running. Her daughter, a writing student, turns them into literary fiction: Though exhausted, she kept running. The student is the daughter. A verbless clause drops the subject and be, keeps the link word (though, although, once, when, if, while) and borrows the subject after the comma. With no link word: Alone at the start line, she… And the writer’s move: Her legs burning, she…

THE COMMON ERRORS

Many languages already drop the subject, so students drop ONLY the subject and keep the verb: Though was exhausted. Also Despite tired (despite needs -ing), In case of doubt (the chunk is If in doubt), Once finishing, the comma splice Her legs burned, she…, and the wrong subject after the comma: Though exhausted, the finish line was still far.

ON SCREEN

The elevation profile of the race, from the start line to the lighthouse: six markers, the runner moving, the sun rising at the end. In scene II the student reads Mom’s note, says it as ONE literary sentence (the first words are given), and taps Check to see the model line. In III the student taps the five verbless clauses in June’s page and finishes the rule. In IV the editor’s page has six lines with one wrong part each; the student says the fix, and the fixed lines build the final page. In V the student taps how to answer the editor (two ways per question), reads the model, and then says their own answer. On the cover the dead words shrink and the word count drops.

A NOTE

Participle clauses (Having missed the train…) are taught at B2. Here the target is the clause with no verb at all, and how writers use it.

THE CONTRACT (VI) AND THE SURPRISE QUESTION

After the editor’s call, the deal. Your student is June, with a secret limit on screen (not less than 200 dollars, and the last page stays); you are the editor, with your own limit that only you see (300 dollars at most, 2,000 words). Ask the three questions in character, wait five seconds in silence after each, and push with the PUSH line (only you see it). They close the deal on screen. There is no right deal. Recast quietly while they talk (Though happy with…, If possible…); at the end ask them to fix one or two sentences themselves.

The Surprise question button (bottom right, only you see it) opens one personal question at any moment. Use it when the lesson has been only exercises for a while.

WOLF IN THIS LESSON

  • Out loud: I You and the Long Run, II The Race Notes, V The Editor Calls (you are the editor), VI The Contract (the editor again, now with a secret limit), VII Your Hard Miles.
  • Focus: III The Rule, IV The Cut.
  • Write: VIII, the hardest thing the student ever finished, in three literary sentences. They go to their My notes.
