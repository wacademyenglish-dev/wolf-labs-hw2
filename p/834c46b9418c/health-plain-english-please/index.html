Plain English, Please · English for Healthcare · Lesson 8 of 16

PREPARE IN 1 MINUTE

What this is: replacing jargon with plain English (hypertension → high blood pressure, benign → not cancer, analgesic → painkiller…), one idea at a time, simple images, and checking with a teach-back (“Can you tell me in your own words…?”).

The story: A Geeky Medics station: a junior doctor explains type 2 diabetes in plain words and asks the patient to summarize. Then jargon pairs and clinical sentences turned into plain English.

About 39 min: I · Explaining Diabetes (9) · II · Memory (8) · III · Say It Simply (11) · IV · The Leaflet Deal (7) · V · I Can (4).

Open with: “Which word do Brazilian patients never understand in your area?” Brazilians also face jargon: the problem is universal.

Many Brazilian professionals know the technical word in English (it comes from Latin) but not the everyday word (painkiller, high blood pressure, broken). This lesson fills that gap. The clip shows 3:04–5:30.

THE LANGUAGE OF THIS LESSON

high blood pressure · not cancer · painkiller · broken bone / a small crack · by mouth / through a drip · Can you tell me in your own words…?

EXPLAINING DIABETES

Geeky Medics, “Type 2 Diabetes Counselling - OSCE Guide”, only 3:04–5:30 (captions checked on 15/10/2026). British English.

MEMORY

A memory game. The student opens two cards; when they match, the student makes a sentence with the pair (example under Show answer).

SAY IT SIMPLY

Use: the student reads a sentence and the clue, and says it the new way. Show the answer after the attempt; accept natural variations. Tap Right for each good one.

I CAN

The student reads each “I can” line out loud and taps the ones they can do. You confirm and stamp the unit. Lines left open go to the homework, and to your next class. Ask the student to list the ten technical words they use most and their plain versions.

THE MISTAKES TO LISTEN FOR

Did you understand? (teach-back instead), hypertension (to a patient), your lesion is benign (the spot is not cancer). B1+: one correction per answer is enough.

THE LEAFLET DEAL (IV) AND THE SURPRISE QUESTION

After saying it simply, the student defends plain English for real. They write a leaflet for new patients (“Your first visit to the physio clinic”); you are Dr. Blake, the director of a fictional clinic, who wants technical words, one page and the leaflet tomorrow. Your secret limit (only you see it) is softer: plain words with the technical word in brackets, two pages with pictures, and Friday. Ask the four questions in order, insist a little, wait five seconds in silence after each, and don’t give your limit first. Then close the three points on screen and use the PUSH line (plain English treats patients like children). Recast quietly while they talk (in plain English, that’s …; what if we …?; Can you tell me in your own words …?); at the end, ask them to fix one or two sentences themselves.

The Surprise question button (bottom right, only you see it) opens one personal question at any moment. Use it when the lesson has been only exercises for a while.
