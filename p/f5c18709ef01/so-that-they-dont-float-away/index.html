So That They Don’t Float Away · The Tethers

PREPARE IN 1 MINUTE

The story: Two hundred and fifty miles above us, a small crew lives in a station that goes around the planet sixteen times a day, and nothing stays where you put it.

The grammar: Who Does the Second Part?: Same person: to. Its own subject: so that. What you avoid: so as not to.

Short class (about 43 min): I You and Your Day · III Life in Space · V A Day on the Station · VI Who Does the Second Part? · VII The Docking Ring · XI Good Night, Station. Skip if you’re short on time: VIII, IX, X (marked EXTRA on your screen). Full lesson: about 63 min.

Open with: “What time does your alarm ring? Do you get up at once, or do you stay in bed?”

Two hundred and fifty miles above us, a small crew lives in a station that goes around the planet sixteen times a day, and nothing stays where you put it. Every strange thing they do has a reason, and the grammar of purpose does the explaining: to / in order to for the same person, so that + can / won’t / could when something else does the second part, and the negative: so as not to / in order not to. The screen is a row of dark portholes: each thing floats, and the right “why” clips a tether to it. The big question: would you live on the station for six months?

A REVIEW LESSON

It works as a follow-up to a lesson on purpose. To / in order to come back quickly; the center of the hour is so that with its own subject and a modal, the past (could / wouldn’t) and the negative. Don’t mention any other lesson to your student.

THE VIDEO

Scene III: a short video about living in space (5:26), sealed until you press play, in four chapters; each stops by itself. The questions before and during are open. The last step is ONE screen: the whole video stays on the left (tap a time to hear that moment again), with six portholes under it; on the right, six True or Lie lines about what the astronaut says. Every right answer clips a tether to one porthole. On a Lie the student says the true line. When all six are done, Alex (the school on the radio) asks one last question (“What do you take to the station, and why?”): the student taps an answer and says the sentence out loud, with to, so that or so as not to.

THE READER

Scene V, before the rule: six short chapters (a made-up crew, Mia and Ken; the facts are real). Your student reads out loud, picks the true sentence, then explains why. Tap exactly what you heard; a correct answer in another shape (“because…”) is fine.

THE PORTUGUESE TRAP

One little word does all the work in Portuguese: purpose before a verb comes out as for keep or for to keep; with a new subject, for it don’t float; the negative, for not or to don’t. After so that, many drop the real verb (so that the glass not break) or keep can in a past story.

THE DOCKING RING

Scene VII: read the moment and the first words, then stop. Moments 2 and 3 are in the past (wouldn’t / could). Moments 1 and 7: not goes before to.

The words (II): gigantic, narrow, survived, homesick and barriers, before the video, in match the columns and hot seat. Five minutes: let the student guess first. In the video (with CC on), these words turn yellow; the explanation comes after the video, in Line by Line. Line by Line (IV, extra): every line of the video; tap one to hear it again, and the lines with yellow words open the explanation.

UNPOPULAR OPINIONS (X) AND THE SURPRISE QUESTION

Four opinions about life on the station and on Earth. Your student taps a number from 1 to 5 and defends it with to, so that or so as not to; then you take the opposite number. There is no right answer: wait five seconds in silence, then use the PUSH line (only you see it) to make the choice harder. Recast mistakes quietly while they talk (watch for “for keep” and “to don’t”); at the end, ask them to fix one or two sentences themselves.

The Surprise question button (bottom right, only you see it) opens one personal question at any moment. Use it when the lesson has been only exercises for a while.
