The Speech · The Shape of a Line

PREPARE IN 1 MINUTE

The story: Two speeches.

The grammar: The Rule: Rhetoric is grammar you can hear: the same shape, again and again.

Short class (about 51 min): I You and the Microphone · III Why the Moon? · V The Sleeping Hall · VI The Rule · VII Ninety Seconds · IX The Town Jury · X The Lamp Stays On. Skip if you’re short on time: VIII (marked EXTRA on your screen). Full lesson: about 58 min.

Open with: “When was the last time you spoke in front of a group? What did your body do?”

Two speeches. The real one: September 12, 1962, a stadium in Texas. A president has eighteen minutes to convince a hot crowd that going to the moon is worth the money, and he does it with pure rhetoric: three-part lists, opposites in the same frame, questions nobody answers, the same words again and again. The invented one: Harlow Falls, a small town where the Lantern Library, open until midnight under a glass dome since 1923, will become a parking garage if the council votes yes on Friday. The student gets ninety seconds at the podium, live on the town radio. Five devices: tricolon, parallelism, antithesis, rhetorical question, anaphora.

THE COMMON ERRORS

School essays punish repetition, so students swap a repeated word for a synonym and kill the anaphora (It opened its doors to me. It welcomed my sister.). Say it clearly: in writing, variety; on a stage, repetition. Also: because is easy (no subject), Why we should…? (no inversion), mixed forms in a list (to read, studying, for meeting), and etc. at the end of a tricolon. Scene V: the student reads the flat line, says it out loud and taps Check (six seats); scene VI: find the broken part of five lines, with the five devices in the corner.

ON SCREEN

When the line comes out right, it draws its shape on the stage: a tricolon as three steps that grow, an anaphora with the repeated start aligned in gold, an antithesis or parallelism as two mirrored halves, a question with the auxiliary lit and a big “?” that nobody answers. In scene VII, the ON AIR meter rises as the student taps each of the six devices while the 90-second clock runs.

THE VIDEO

Scene III: four short chapters from the 1962 moon speech (public domain), sealed until the first play. Before it, the student chooses how to open the speech and slides a ruler; in each chapter the student taps True or Lie (the ▶ replays the line); afterwards the student matches each device to a line of the speech. The speech is about space, not about a party: keep the focus on HOW he speaks.

The words (II): delighted, progress, challenge, ignorance and available, before the video, in mystery meaning and the word in your life. Five minutes: let the student guess first. In the video (with CC on), these words turn yellow; the explanation comes after the video, in Line by Line. Line by Line (IV, extra): every line of the video; tap one to hear it again, and the lines with yellow words open the explanation.

THE TOWN JURY (IX) AND THE SURPRISE QUESTION

Scene IX is now a jury with evidence: after the five-to-two vote, the student decides who pays for the glass dome, whether Councillor Reyes’ no vote was reasonable, and, back to the video, whether the moon was worth the money. They read the evidence, choose a verdict and say why with one device (an antithesis, a rhetorical question, a three-part list). There is no right answer. Wait five seconds in silence, then use the PUSH line (only you see it) to make it harder. Recast quietly while they talk; at the end, ask them to fix one or two sentences themselves.

The Surprise question button (only you see it) opens one personal question at any moment. Use it when the lesson has been only exercises for a while.
